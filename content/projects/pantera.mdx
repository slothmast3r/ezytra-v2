<Chapter id="design" title="Design">

The hardest part of Pantera was not the layout — it was deciding who the homepage was for. A martial arts club in Mokotów has at least three audiences that share almost nothing in common: parents looking for a safe place to send a seven-year-old, adults wanting practical self-defense, and seniors interested in low-impact movement. The three groups arrive on the page asking different questions, in different vocabulary, with different fears. A single hero either picks one audience and ignores the others, or tries to address all three and ends up sounding generic — the same as every other sports school in Warsaw. The decision was to stop pretending the homepage could be one thing. Instead, the page asks one question — *which class are you here for?* — and routes immediately. Three cards, real training photos, audience-specific copy from that point forward. Everything below assumes you have already made that choice.

The hero above this routing decision had to do its own job: prove that this is a real club, not a stock-photo facade. The choice was a 10-second video loop instead of photography, edited by hand into three beats — kids having fun, adult Krav Maga, Tai Chi — in that specific order. Kids open the loop because they are the largest audience segment; a parent visiting the page sees their child within the first three seconds. The visual mix mirrors the actual class mix at the club. It is not a montage that could belong to any sports school.

Two earlier directions were explored and discarded. The first was a torn-paper SVG divider between the hero and the first content section, intended to add visual dynamism. It conflicted with the brand's geometric, sharp visual language, and at the execution quality achievable without a custom illustrator, it read as a generic template element. The second was a uniform 3-column grid repeated for offers, instructors, reviews, and pricing. Convenient to build, fatal to scan: every section had identical visual weight, so the page communicated that nothing mattered more than anything else. The final layout uses a deliberate variation in rhythm — a 50/50 split for the USP, a counter-scrolling marquee for reviews, a standard 3-column grid for instructors. The variation itself signals hierarchy.

Reviews are presented as two horizontal marquees scrolling in opposite directions, with pause-on-hover for accessibility. A static grid of six testimonials would communicate "here are all our reviews." An infinite marquee communicates "there are too many to count" — which is the accurate framing for a club with over a hundred Google reviews. Pricing follows a similar logic-to-form mapping: three tiers, no "most popular" badge. The badge is a SaaS pattern that works when clicking a plan leads to checkout. At Pantera there is no checkout — every CTA leads to "Skontaktuj się" or "Zapisz się," every path goes through a human. The first tier reads "Zapytaj o cenę," removing the price entirely for B2B and corporate clients to prevent premature price objections on high-LTV contracts. Instructors are kept as a section despite the conventional format because in martial arts, students choose the instructor before the club. Real names, real faces, real credentials remove the abstraction — a parent is no longer enrolling in "Pantera," they are trusting their child to Michał Jaworski.

</Chapter>

<Chapter id="performance" title="Performance">

The redesign reaches a Lighthouse score of 94 / 92 / 100 / 100, up from 83 / 86 / 73 / 85. The headline numbers are useful, but the most meaningful gain is the metric that does not appear in marketing: Cumulative Layout Shift dropping from 0.235 to 0.015. CLS is the only Core Web Vital users actually feel — visible jumps in content as the page loads, the button that moves under your finger as you tap it, the image that pushes the headline downward. The old site was sitting near Google's "poor" threshold of 0.25. The new one is comfortably inside the "good" range, a 94% improvement.

What fixed CLS was unglamorous: every image now has explicit width and height attributes so the browser reserves space before the bytes arrive; display fonts are preloaded with `font-display: swap` instead of letting them block render; and the WordPress theme CSS that loaded asynchronously and competed with the page during render is simply gone. These are not optimizations in the clever sense. They are the absence of choices the previous theme made by accident.

The Lighthouse jump from 83 to 94 in performance is partly structural, not just optimization. Static generation means the public pages are pre-rendered HTML with no PHP execution per request and no database query on page load. The video hero is heavier than the old static image, but the rest of the page is faster by enough margin that net load time still falls inside Google's "good" thresholds. The 100/100 in Best Practices and SEO is a quieter win: clean dependency management, proper HTTPS on every asset, schema.org markup for LocalBusiness and SportsActivityLocation — none of which existed on the old site, all of which compound over time in organic search.

</Chapter>

<Chapter id="stack" title="Stack">

Replacing WordPress was not necessary purely for performance — it was necessary for control. Shared hosting cannot run Node.js or PostgreSQL, schema lives inside the host's database with no migrations and no version control, and every plugin is a third-party dependency operating outside your codebase. The new stack moves all of those concerns into the project: collections defined in TypeScript, schema migrations handled by Payload, deployment and SSL handled by Coolify on a Hetzner VPS. Performance is the visible win; the structural win is that nothing about content management or hosting requires me to log into someone else's dashboard to do my job.

Payload was chosen over the obvious alternatives for specific reasons. Sanity is excellent but charges per editor seat at scale, and for a single-location club where the staff who edit content might grow from one to four people over a few years, that pricing model is hostile in the long term. Strapi is closer to Payload in shape but rougher around the edges, and its v5 migration discouraged committing to it for a production build that needs to last. Contentful is mature and expensive in a way that makes no sense at this size. Payload is open source, self-hosted, type-safe by default, and the admin UI is significantly cleaner than WordPress for non-technical users — no plugin menu, no theme customizer, no fifteen-year-old UX debt. The client sees only the collections they need, with field labels in Polish.

Hosting on Hetzner via Coolify rather than Vercel was a deliberate trade-off. Vercel is the path of least resistance for Next.js — push to git, get a deploy. The catch is that Vercel's pricing scales with traffic and bandwidth in ways that punish video-heavy sites the moment they become successful, and the abstractions hide the underlying server in a way that becomes painful when you need to debug something specific. Coolify on a €5/month Hetzner VPS gives you Vercel-like ergonomics — automatic deploys from git, built-in reverse proxy, automatic SSL — on a flat-rate machine you fully control. It is more setup up front in exchange for predictable cost and a server that does not surprise you. For a project that is also a template for future agency clients, that predictability matters more than the convenience of zero configuration.

</Chapter>
